Signs the leak is in the service line

A service line leak behaves differently from one inside the house.

  • 1A wet patch or sunken spot in the yard. Water finds the easiest path up, which is often near the line.
  • 2Pressure that fades across the whole house. A large leak before the house steals pressure from every tap.
  • 3A meter that runs after you close the main. That places the leak between the meter and the house.
  • 4Muddy or discolored water. A break lets soil enter the pipe when pressure drops.

Repair, replace or trenchless

A single break in sound pipe is a spot repair. A line that has failed more than once, or is made of corroded galvanized steel, is better replaced. Trenchless methods pull a new line through the old path with only two small pits, which suits driveways and mature landscaping.

Call 811 before any digging. The free locate marks gas, electric and water lines. In South Carolina, the one-call center is the first step on any excavation.

Who owns which part of the line

The utility generally owns the main and the connection to the meter. The homeowner generally owns the line from the meter to the house. Policies vary by utility, so confirm yours.

Cayce and West Columbia run their own water systems, and the Town of Lexington buys water from the City of West Columbia. The utility that supplies your tap is the one to call about the street side.

Pressure and the water heater

A service line problem can show up at the water heater first. Pressure spikes can trip the relief valve, and low pressure can starve a tankless unit. If your heater misbehaves along with the faucets, read our page on water heater repair after the line is checked.

Emergency water line repair: no water, a soggy yard or a meter that will not stop

A failed service line announces itself loudly. Water bubbling up in the yard, a swampy patch with no rain, no water at the house or a meter that spins with every valve closed all point to the pipe between the meter and the home.

Close the valve at the house, then check whether the meter still moves. If it does, call the emergency line. In most cases the utility handles the meter and the street side while the owner handles the line past the meter, and a quick call tells us which part needs digging.

We repair or replace the damaged run, restore the shutoff and test pressure before backfill, so water is back on the same visit whenever the dig allows.

Replacing the line from the meter to the house: what callers want to know

Many calls here are quote requests. Owners ask what it costs to replace the line from the meter, how long the dig takes and what to do with a line that runs hundreds of feet. Length, depth, soil, driveway crossings and pipe material all change the answer.

Older homes may still have lead or galvanized service lines. Ask your utility what it has on record for your address, because a line replacement is the right time to remove old material. Trenchless methods can avoid cutting a driveway or garden when the route and soil allow.

A written scope should state the pipe type, the depth, how the shutoff and meter connection are handled and who restores the landscaping.

Leaks under driveways, between buildings and out to the barn

Some leaks hide under concrete or run between outbuildings. Callers describe a leak under a driveway, a line from a well to a barn, an underground pipe out to a pole, and multi-unit properties where nobody knows which line is losing water.

We trace the route with a line locator, isolate sections with valves and use listening gear on the pipe. Plastic pipe is quieter than metal, so some jobs need a pressure test or tracer gas to narrow the spot. A saw cut is the last step, not the first.

No water or weak pressure at the house: what to check before you call

No water for a day, low pressure in every bathroom or a house that went dry while the bill is paid are common calls. Start with the simple checks: is the main valve fully open, do neighbors have water and does the meter show movement.

A partly closed valve, a stuck pressure regulator, a leaking service line or a failed well pressure tank can cause the same symptom. On a well, check the pump breaker first. If neighbors are dry too, the problem is probably with the utility.

Low pressure that hits only the hot side points to the water heater, and one weak fixture points to its own valve or aerator. Tell us which, and we can bring the right tools.

Reading your meter and checking your pressure

Most meters have a flow indicator that spins when any water moves. Find it before you need it, and note the reading at night and again in the morning. If the number changes while no one used water, you have a leak.

A pressure regulator near the main keeps house pressure in a safe range. Water pressure above 80 pounds per square inch is above what the International Plumbing Code (IPC) allows at fixtures, and below about 40 feels weak. A cheap gauge on an outdoor spigot gives you a number.

The meter is the line between you and the utility

Columbia Water owns the main and the meter, and the pipe to your house is yours. If the leak is near the street, call (803) 545-3300 first. In Cayce and West Columbia, the city utility plays that role, and some Lexington County homes use private wells with no utility meter at all. The Broad River Diversion Canal and Lake Murray feed Columbia Water, so street-side water problems go to its customer care line.

Long service lines in Hopkins, Chapin and Blythewood

Acreage lots in Hopkins and Blythewood and the sloped lots around Chapin mean a long run between the meter and the house. A leak on that run shows up as a soft patch, a high bill or low pressure long before water surfaces.

In Chapin, the town owns water lines only in part of town and the City of Columbia operates the rest, so who fixes the street side depends on the address.
