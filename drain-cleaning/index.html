Cable, jet or camera: how a technician chooses

A cable clears a soft clog such as hair and paper. It punches a hole through a blockage, which is why grease and roots often return a few weeks later. Hydro jetting scours the pipe wall with high-pressure water and handles grease and root growth better.

When a drain clogs a second time, a camera is the honest next step. It shows whether the cause is a belly in the pipe, a broken joint or roots, and it turns a guess into a repair plan.

What should never go down the drain

Most clogs we see have a cause that is easy to avoid once you know it.

  • 1Cooking grease. It cools and coats the pipe wall. Pour it into a can, not the sink.
  • 2Wipes labeled flushable. They do not break down like paper and are a leading cause of main line clogs.
  • 3Coffee grounds and rice. They pack into traps and slow the flow.
  • 4Paper towels and feminine products. They are built to hold together when wet.
  • 5Harsh drain openers. Caustic chemicals can damage old pipe and are dangerous for the person who opens the line next.

Old lines, big lots and tree roots in the Columbia area

Mid-century neighborhoods like Forest Acres have long laterals on large lots with mature trees, and roots find joints in clay and cast iron. In the older mill-village blocks of West Columbia, branch lines may be a century old.

If roots are the cause, cleaning is a temporary fix and a repair is the permanent one. Our page on sewer line repair explains the options once a camera confirms the damage.

Backups after heavy rain

In October 2015 one gauge on Gills Creek logged 10.64 inches of rain in four hours. Saturated ground and overloaded public sewers can push water back into low fixtures, and a drain that gurgles only during storms points to a sewer that cannot keep up.

A backwater valve on the lateral is the permanent protection for a low-lying lot. Until it is installed, avoid running water during storms and call if sewage appears.

What a drain cleaning visit looks like

The technician asks which fixtures are affected, finds the nearest cleanout and chooses a method. For a sink, that is usually a hand cable through the trap. For a main line, it is a powered cable or a jet run from the cleanout, often with a camera pass afterward to confirm the line is clear.

You should leave with a clear explanation of the cause, what was removed and what to watch for. The International Plumbing Code (IPC) calls for accessible cleanouts on building drains, so a missing cleanout is a defect and not just an inconvenience. A drain that clears once but returns is a symptom, not a fix.

Know where your cleanout is

A cleanout is a capped pipe that gives direct access to the main line, often near the foundation, in a crawlspace or outside near the house. Finding it before an emergency saves time and money. Without one, a technician may have to pull a toilet to reach the line.

Mark the cap with a flag or a photo on your phone. If your house has no cleanout, adding one is a small project that pays for itself the next time the line backs up.

Keeping drains flowing between visits

Flush kitchen drains with hot water after washing greasy dishes and use a strainer in tubs and showers. Run cold water when using a disposal, and put grease in the trash. Twice a year, run a long cleaning pass in any line that has clogged before.

If the same drain clogs more than twice in a year, ask for a camera look, because a repeat clog is a pipe problem.

Emergency drain cleaning: gurgling toilets, flooded floors and a main line that will not drain

Some clogs cannot wait. A toilet that bubbles when the washer drains, sewage in a shower, water flooding a bathroom floor or a four-inch main that has stopped moving all call for a same-day response. Stop using water, because every flush sends more into the blockage.

When you call, tell us which fixtures are backing up and where the cleanout is. A backup at the lowest drain in the house usually means the main line is blocked, while one slow sink means a branch. That single detail changes the tool we bring.

If a call comes late at night, we explain what can be done then and what waits for morning. A main-line clog gets priority at any hour because standing wastewater is not safe to leave in a house.

Kitchen sinks, showers and tubs: slow drains that keep coming back

Kitchen drains clog from grease, food and soap that cool and stick inside the pipe. A double sink that backs up into the laundry sink is a branch problem, and a sink that drains slowly after every wash points to a coating that a plunger will not remove.

Shower and tub drains clog with hair and soap scum, usually close to the drain where a short cable clears them. If the tub also gurgles when another fixture runs, the blockage may sit farther down, and a longer cable or a camera check is the right next step.

Chemical drain openers can damage old pipes and leave caustic liquid that is unsafe for the technician. Skip them and tell us what you already tried.

Snaked it yourself first? What to tell the technician

Many callers have already run a store-bought snake, and that history helps. Tell us how far the cable went, whether it met a hard stop or a soft one and whether the drain ran freely for a day or a week afterward.

A hard stop at a consistent length often means a joint, a sag or a root mass. A soft stop that clears and returns points to grease. A cable that goes in easily while the drain stays slow can mean the line is partly collapsed, and a camera shows what the cable cannot.

What to do when sewage backs up: safety steps

Sewage carries bacteria, so the first steps protect people before they protect floors.

  • 1Stop using water. Do not flush, run the washer or use the dishwasher, because everything you send down comes back up.
  • 2Keep people and pets out. Close off the room and keep children and animals away from the affected area.
  • 3Avoid contact. Do not wade through it barefoot or handle soaked items without gloves and boots.
  • 4Never mix cleaning chemicals. Combining bleach with other cleaners can release dangerous fumes.
  • 5Photograph the damage, then call. Photos help your insurer, and a camera inspection finds the blockage.

Odors from drains: traps, vents and dry floor drains

A sewer smell in a bathroom often comes from a dry trap. A seldom-used tub or floor drain loses its water seal, and gas rises through the drain. Running water for a minute usually restores the seal. If the smell returns, a blocked plumbing vent or a failing wax ring may be the cause.

Gurgling toilets together with an odor suggest a vent or main-line problem. A camera and a vent check will separate the two.

Rental and multi-unit properties

In a shared building, one tenant's slow drain can be the first sign of a main line problem that affects everyone downstream. Landlords should document each call, note which units are affected, and schedule a camera look when two or more units report the same issue. That records the cause for insurance and for anyone who disputes responsibility.

Sewer service across the river and the county line

Columbia Water collects wastewater inside the city, while Cayce runs its own regional plant, which opened in October 2012. Which utility owns the main under your street decides who answers a street-side backup. Low ground near the Congaree Riverwalk in Lexington County and along Gills Creek in Richland County is where flood history makes sewer backups a bigger concern.

Pipe sizes decide the tool

A kitchen sink branch is usually 1.5 to 2 inches across, a bathroom branch is about 2 inches, and a main drain is typically 3 or 4 inches. A small cable fits a sink line, while a main line takes a longer, heavier cable or a jetting hose, which is why the clog's location sets both the method and the price.

What we ask before a technician heads out

Tell dispatch which fixture is slow or blocked and whether others are affected. One blocked sink is a branch problem, while a tub that fills when the washer drains points to the main line. A floor drain that bubbles in a storm is a different story from a toilet that gurgles.

Say whether the clog has come back before and how long ago it was last cleared. Those answers decide whether a cable is enough or whether a camera comes along on the first visit.

Clogs in the old streetcar suburbs

Shandon, Earlewood and Elmwood Park were laid out between about 1890 and 1910, and Elmwood Park was annexed in 1907 as the city's first suburb. Clay and cast iron laterals from that era collect roots and scale, so a main line clog there is more often a pipe problem than a one-time blockage.

A cable clears the stoppage, and a camera run afterward shows whether the pipe is cracked or offset. Repeated clogs in these neighborhoods usually justify the camera on the first visit.
