Tank or Tankless Water Heater: Which Fits a Columbia Home?

Wall-mounted on-demand heaters never run out, but they pull a lot of gas at once. This comparison weighs footprint, flow, venting and fuel for houses across the Midlands.

Written by the Columbia 24/7 Plumbing team. Published October 3, 2026. Updated October 12, 2026.

The basic difference

A tank heater keeps a reservoir of hot water ready, so it works from a modest gas line or a simple electric circuit. A tankless heater heats water as you use it, which means it needs a lot of energy at the moment you open the tap. That one fact explains most of the differences in cost and installation.

Neither type is better for every house. The choice depends on household size, the space available, the fuel supply and how you use hot water.

The gas line question

Gas tankless units draw far more fuel than a tank. A house with a half-inch line sized for a tank, a range and a dryer may not have enough capacity, and the unit starves under load. The symptom is a heater that fires and then shuts off when two fixtures run at once.

A good installer compares the line size to the unit's rating and follows the International Fuel Gas Code (IFGC) as South Carolina adopts it. Our page on gas line installation and repair explains the work, and our page on tankless water heater service covers maintenance.

Soft city water changes the maintenance story

Columbia Water puts its supply near 1 to 2 grains per gallon, which counts as soft. Scale builds more slowly in a heat exchanger in that water than in a hard-water market, so descaling intervals can be longer than the national averages suggest. Follow the manufacturer's schedule to keep the warranty valid.

A private well or another utility's water can be much harder. Test it before you assume the same schedule applies.

Who benefits most from tankless

Households with high hot-water demand spread across the day tend to like tankless. Smaller homes with limited space and owners who plan to stay for years often see the payoff. Newer houses in places like Lexington often have a gas stub that can be upgraded.

Households that want the lowest purchase price, or have an electric supply and a small hot-water load, often do better with a tank.

A decision checklist

Answer these before you choose.

  • 1What fuel do you have? Gas tankless needs a large line. Electric tankless needs heavy wiring.
  • 2How many fixtures run at once? A flow-rate estimate tells you the unit size.
  • 3How long will you stay? A longer horizon favors tankless.
  • 4Where does the unit go? Venting and clearance limit options.
  • 5What does the quote include? Gas line, venting, permit and haul-away should be itemized.

Thinking about cost over the life of the unit

A tankless heater costs more at the start, so the question is whether it pays back. Savings come from lower standby losses and a longer life, and they depend on how much hot water you use and what you pay for gas or electricity. A household that uses little hot water will recover the extra cost slowly, and one that uses a lot may recover it sooner.

Do not forget the installation line. A larger gas line, new venting or an electrical upgrade can add to the price, and a good quote itemizes each piece. Ask for the numbers before you compare.

Wait time and recirculation

Tankless heaters deliver continuous hot water, but they do not remove the wait for hot water to travel through the pipes. If the heater is far from the bathroom, you still wait for the cold water in the line to clear. A recirculation pump keeps hot water moving and shortens the wait, at the price of some energy.

Some units have a small internal buffer tank, which reduces the cold-water sandwich effect on short draws. Ask about it if you notice that problem.

Warranty questions to ask

The warranty tells you what the maker expects from the install.

  • 1Who pays for labor? Many warranties cover parts only.
  • 2What maintenance is required? Some void coverage without proof of flushing.
  • 3Is the heat exchanger covered longer? It is the costliest part.
  • 4Does a professional install matter? Some warranties require it.

Other options: heat pump and point-of-use heaters

A heat pump water heater moves heat from the surrounding air into the tank. It uses much less electricity than a standard electric tank, but it needs space, a drain for condensation and a room that stays above freezing. A small point-of-use heater serves a single distant sink and can end a long wait for hot water.

Rebates and tax credits come and go, so check what applies at the time you buy, and ask your installer to put any assumed incentives in writing.

Hot water demand in numbers

A shower uses about 2 gallons per minute, a kitchen faucet about 1.5, and a dishwasher a few gallons per cycle. A typical tank holds 40 to 50 gallons, with the first-hour rating on the label showing how much it can deliver in a busy hour. A tankless unit is rated by flow instead, which is why two showers at once decide its size.

Replacing in an emergency versus planning ahead

A tank that fails at night usually gets replaced with another tank, because there is no time to upgrade a gas line. If you suspect your heater is near the end, planning ahead lets you choose. Our page on water heater installation and replacement explains sizing and installation.

Gas, electric or propane on your street

Whether tankless makes sense often starts with the fuel at your address. Natural gas service does not reach every road around the Midlands, so ask your gas utility whether your street has it before you price a gas unit. Without it you are choosing among electric, propane and heat pump models.

Houses near Lexington and in West Columbia vary a lot on this point. A technician can check the gas line size on a visit, and tankless water heater service or water heater replacement covers whichever you choose.
