Does Home Insurance Cover a Burst Pipe or Slab Leak in Columbia, SC?

The answer is often yes for a sudden burst and often no for a leak that went on for months. The details sit in your policy, and what you document can matter as much as what you file.

Written by the Columbia 24/7 Plumbing team. Published October 3, 2026. Updated October 12, 2026.

A note on what this page is

This is general information, not insurance or legal advice. Policies differ by company, state and endorsement. Your policy and your agent decide what is covered. The goal here is to help you ask better questions and keep better records.

Sudden versus gradual

Most homeowners policies cover damage from a sudden and accidental event, such as a supply line that bursts and floods a room. Many exclude damage from long-term leaking, seepage or wear and tear, since those are seen as maintenance problems.

That one distinction explains most claim outcomes. A pipe that bursts on Tuesday is a different claim from a drip that has stained a ceiling for a year. The wording in your policy around continuous or repeated seepage is worth reading.

Slab leaks need special attention

Slab leaks are hard to classify. The pipe fails in a place nobody can see, and the damage can build up for a long time. Some policies cover the cost of tearing out part of the slab to reach the leak, and others do not. Ask your agent about access and tear-out language before you need it.

Keep the evidence: the meter readings, the detection report and the plumber's written explanation. Our page on slab leak detection and repair explains what that report should show, and homes in places like Forest Acres are typical examples of slab-and-older-pipe houses.

What to document

Good records speed claims and reduce disputes.

  • 1Photographs and video. Capture the damage before you move or throw out anything.
  • 2A written cause from the plumber. The invoice should say what failed and why.
  • 3Dated meter readings. They show when the loss began.
  • 4A list of damaged belongings. Include age and approximate value.
  • 5Receipts for emergency steps. Mitigation costs are often part of the claim.

Your duty to limit the damage

Policies commonly require you to take reasonable steps to prevent further damage, such as shutting off the water and starting drying. Waiting can reduce what a policy pays. Our pages on burst pipe repair and water mitigation explain the first-day steps.

Keep receipts and notes from any emergency work, and tell your insurer early. They can tell you what they will want to see and sometimes recommend steps.

Deductibles and whether to file

A small loss may cost less than your deductible, and a claim can raise your premium or affect renewal. Before you file, get a repair estimate and compare it with the deductible. Your agent can tell you how claims are recorded and what a no-claim inquiry looks like.

Do not delay mitigation to decide. Stop the water and start drying, and keep the receipts, because those costs often count toward the claim whether you file now or later.

Working with an adjuster and a restoration company

The insurer, the contractor and you each have a role.

  • 1Report the loss promptly. Most policies require timely notice.
  • 2Ask what documentation the adjuster wants. Photos, moisture logs and invoices are typical.
  • 3Get written estimates. Both for drying and for repairs.
  • 4Keep the damaged materials if asked. An adjuster may want to see them.
  • 5Read what you sign. Assignment of benefits forms and work authorizations carry consequences.

Prevention that insurers like

Some insurers offer discounts for leak sensors or automatic shutoff valves, and nearly all appreciate a pressure regulator and braided hoses. These devices reduce the chance of a claim and the size of one if it happens.

Even without a discount, the cost of a sensor is small against a flooded floor. Ask your agent which devices they recognize.

Renters, landlords and condo owners

Who holds which policy decides who files. Check your own situation.

SituationUsually coversUsually does not
Renter with renters insuranceYour belongings and some living expensesThe building or the pipes
Landlord with a rental policyThe building structureThe tenant's belongings
Condo ownerInterior finishes and belongings, depending on the master policyShared pipes inside walls, which the association handles
HomeownerThe structure and belongings, subject to exclusionsWear, long-term seepage and flood

In a rental, report a leak to the landlord in writing as soon as you notice it. Written notice protects you and starts the repair clock.

Numbers worth checking in your policy

Homeowners deductibles commonly range from several hundred dollars to a couple of thousand, and some policies set a separate, higher deductible for wind or water. Flood coverage through the National Flood Insurance Program generally carries a 30-day waiting period before it starts. Read the declarations page for your deductible, your dwelling limit and the sewer backup limit if you have one.

Questions to ask your agent today

A call before a loss costs nothing.

  • 1Do I have sewer backup or water backup coverage? Many policies need an endorsement.
  • 2How is a slab leak handled? Ask about tear-out and access.
  • 3What is my deductible for water damage? Some policies have separate deductibles.
  • 4Do I have flood coverage? A separate policy is usually required, often with a waiting period.
  • 5What does the policy say about long-term seepage? Ask for the exact exclusion wording.

Insurance help in South Carolina

The South Carolina Department of Insurance runs a Consumer Services Division for questions about a claim. Its guidance is to tell your agent as soon as possible, photograph or video your belongings and keep receipts for temporary repairs. It also notes that a standard homeowners policy leaves out flood damage.

Keep the paperwork for a leak at hand when the adjuster arrives. In Forest Acres and Irmo, a fast call to burst pipe repair or slab leak repair limits damage, and water mitigation documents the drying.
