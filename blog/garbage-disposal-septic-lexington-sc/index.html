Garbage Disposals and Septic Tanks in Lexington, SC

Ground food scraps join the sludge in a buried tank. Whether the convenience is worth it depends on tank size, pumping habits and what you feed the unit.

Written by the Columbia 24/7 Plumbing team. Published October 3, 2026. Updated October 12, 2026.

Are you on septic or city sewer?

Many homes in the Columbia area are on city sewer, but plenty of rural and lakeside properties in Lexington and Richland counties use septic tanks. If you are not sure, check your bill. A monthly sewer charge points to a public system, and the absence of one suggests a tank in the yard.

The answer matters because a septic system is a small treatment plant, and what you put down the drain shapes how it performs. A disposal sends a steady stream of food solids into that system.

How a septic system works

Wastewater flows into a buried tank, where solids settle to the bottom as sludge and grease floats as scum. Clearer water in the middle drains to a field where soil treats it. Bacteria in the tank break down some of the solids, but not all of them.

When sludge and scum build too high, solids can reach the drain field and clog it. A clogged field is expensive to repair, which is why people on septic pay attention to what goes in.

What a disposal changes

Ground food scraps are still solids, and they add to the tank. Here is how that plays out.

EffectWithout a disposalWith a disposal
Solids entering the tankLowerHigher, finely ground food adds to sludge
Pumping frequencyStandard scheduleOften more frequent
Grease and fatsSink habits matterGrinding does not remove grease
Bacterial balanceSteadyHeavy loads can strain it

What to keep out of the disposal on septic

If you keep a disposal, treat it as an occasional tool, not a trash can.

  • 1Grease, oil and fat. They float as scum and coat the pipes and field.
  • 2Coffee grounds and eggshells in quantity. They add dense solids.
  • 3Fibrous scraps. Celery, corn husks and onion skins tangle and settle.
  • 4Starches in bulk. Rice, pasta and potato peels swell and pack.
  • 5Harsh chemicals. Strong cleaners and bleach in large amounts can harm the bacteria.

Pumping schedules

The Environmental Protection Agency suggests that households inspect their septic systems about every three years and pump them every three to five years, depending on size and use. A household with a disposal, a large family or a small tank may need to pump more often. A septic professional can measure sludge and scum and recommend a schedule.

Plumbers handle the pipes and the disposal. A septic contractor handles the tank, the field and the pumping, and we are upfront about that line.

Signs the system is struggling

Septic trouble usually announces itself. These are the signs to watch.

Slow drains throughout the house

Several fixtures draining slowly can mean a full tank or a clogged field.

Gurgling pipes

Air pushed back through the lines suggests a blockage.

Wet or spongy ground over the field

Untreated water is rising to the surface.

Sewage odor near the tank

The tank or its lid may be failing.

Septic or sewer: more ways to tell

A property tax or utility record may list your sewer service. Your city or county health office keeps permit records for septic systems. A visible lid or a riser in the yard, usually a round or square cover, suggests a tank. Water usage records alone do not tell you, but a missing sewer charge on your bill does.

If you bought the house recently, your closing papers or home inspection may include a septic inspection. If you cannot find a record, a septic contractor can locate the tank with a probe.

Protect the drain field

The field is the costly part, so treat it with care.

  • 1Keep vehicles off it. Weight compacts soil and crushes pipes.
  • 2Plant grass, not trees. Roots seek water and enter lines.
  • 3Spread out laundry. Many loads in one day can overwhelm the system.
  • 4Fix leaks. A running toilet adds water all day.
  • 5Divert roof drains away. Extra water can saturate the field.

Septic tank safety: what to do and what never to do

A septic tank is a confined space with hazardous gas. Treat it with respect.

  • 1Never climb into a tank. Hydrogen sulfide and methane can overcome a person in seconds, and many deaths happen to would-be rescuers.
  • 2Keep lids secure. A missing or cracked lid is a fall hazard for children and pets.
  • 3Do not use a lighter or flame near an open tank. Methane is flammable.
  • 4Leave pumping and repairs to professionals. They have gas monitors and the right equipment.
  • 5Call for help at the first sign of failure. Standing sewage on the ground should be avoided until it is addressed.

Alternatives to grinding

On a septic system, the simplest option is to scrape plates into the trash or a compost bin and skip the disposal. Compost keeps food waste out of the tank, and it makes useful soil for a garden. A sink strainer catches the rest.

If you love your disposal and have city sewer, nothing here applies. If you are on septic and keep one, grind small amounts with cold water running, and keep a closer eye on the pumping schedule.

How common septic is

The Environmental Protection Agency reports that about one in five American households uses a septic system, and the share is higher in rural parts of the country. EPA guidance suggests an inspection at least every three years and pumping every three to five years, which a heavy disposal user should treat as the short end of that range.

If you are replacing a disposal

If a disposal needs replacing on a septic home, consider whether you really want to keep it. A strainer and a compost bin cost less than a premature pump-out. If you keep one, choose a model with a good motor and use it lightly. Our page on garbage disposal repair and installation covers the installation details. For a kitchen line that clogs repeatedly, drain cleaning is the first step.
