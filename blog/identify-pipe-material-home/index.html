How to Identify Pipe Material in a Columbia, SC Home

Color, stamped codes and a magnet separate galvanized steel, copper, CPVC, PEX and gray polybutylene. Five minutes at the heater or a sink cabinet usually answers it.

Written by the Columbia 24/7 Plumbing team. Published October 3, 2026.

Where to look

Start where the pipes are exposed. The best places are around the water heater, near the main shutoff where the line enters the house, under kitchen and bathroom sinks, and in the crawlspace or garage. Look at the part of the pipe, not at a fitting or a valve, since fittings are often a different material.

Take photographs with a phone flashlight. If you call a plumber later, a clear picture of the pipe and any printed markings answers half the questions on the first call.

Polybutylene: the one to take seriously

Polybutylene was widely installed from the late 1970s into the mid-1990s and is known for failing at fittings. Look for markings that include PB or the code PB2110 printed along the pipe. The plastic fittings and copper crimp rings at connections are another giveaway.

Not every house with polybutylene has leaked, but a failure can send a lot of water into a wall. Houses built in that window, including many in Irmo, are worth checking near the water heater and main shutoff. If you find it, ask about repiping before a fitting decides the timing for you.

Galvanized steel and what its age means

Galvanized steel is coated with zinc, and that coating wears away from the inside. A magnet tells you quickly, and so does a scratch with a key, which shows dull gray steel instead of copper-colored metal. Brown water that clears after a minute and weak pressure at older fixtures are the classic signs.

Galvanized pipe commonly lasts 40 to 50 years. Mid-century neighborhoods like Forest Acres fit that window, so a scratch test is worth doing before you fix one leak at a time.

Copper, CPVC and PEX: the later generations

Copper has the longest track record. It can develop pinholes in some water conditions and its joints can fail, but it generally ages well. CPVC is a cream-colored plastic that can turn brittle with age and heat. PEX is flexible, handles cold well and uses fewer joints, which is why many modern repipes use it.

Columbia Water is soft and disinfects with chloramine. Both facts are compatible with all three materials, though the rubber seals in fittings and fixtures are where chloramine tends to show wear.

Drain pipes are a different story

Supply pipes bring water in. Drain pipes carry it out, and they are made of other materials. Black, heavy cast iron appears in mid-century homes. White or gray PVC is common in newer ones. Clay pipe and fiber conduit sold as Orangeburg pipe appear in older sewer laterals. If a drain keeps clogging, a camera look at the lateral tells you which one you have. Why mixed metals matter

Where copper meets galvanized steel, the two metals can react and corrode, especially when water moves through them. Plumbers use dielectric unions or special fittings to separate them. A fitting that looks green or white and crusty at a transition is a sign of this reaction.

If you find a transition with heavy corrosion, mark it with a photo and mention it when you talk to a plumber. These spots often leak first.

When to pay for a professional opinion

You can identify most pipe by eye, but a plumber can see more. A camera in a wall cavity or a pressure test can show corrosion you cannot see. Ask for one if you are buying an older house or planning a remodel that opens walls. Our page on leak detection explains how hidden problems are found.

Taking photos a plumber can use

Use the flash, shoot from two angles and include something for scale, such as a coin or a tape measure. Capture any printed text on the pipe, the fittings at each end and the shutoff valve. A wide shot showing where the pipe sits in the house helps just as much as a close-up.

How long each material tends to last

Galvanized steel commonly lasts 40 to 50 years. Copper commonly lasts 50 years or more, though water chemistry and workmanship change that. PEX has been installed widely since the 1990s, and gray polybutylene was used from roughly 1978 to 1995. Matching your pipe to the right row tells you how much of its life is left.

If your pipes sit near the end of that range, document what you find with photos, ask your insurer whether your policy treats old plumbing differently. Then ask a plumber for a repair or repipe price and a schedule before a leak sets one for you.

What to do with what you find

Your findings decide the next step.

  • 1Copper, CPVC or PEX in good condition. Nothing to do. Note the material for your records.
  • 2Galvanized steel with brown water or low pressure. Plan a repipe consultation.
  • 3Polybutylene. Ask a plumber about replacement and check your insurance policy.
  • 4A mix of materials. Look closely at the transitions, because dissimilar metals corrode where they meet.
