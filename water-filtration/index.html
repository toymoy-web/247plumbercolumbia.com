Water Filtration Installation in Columbia, SC

Why chloramine changes the filter you need

Columbia Water has used chloramine as a disinfectant at its Canal plant since the mid-1960s and at its Lake Murray plant since 1982. Chloramine is more stable than chlorine, so it does not simply flash off in a basic carbon filter.

Catalytic carbon or a longer contact time is what removes it. A filter sold only as a chlorine filter may do little for chloramine taste, and the replacement schedule matters just as much.

A filter is not a boil-water fix

During the October 2015 flood the Columbia Canal breached and the city issued a boil-water advisory. A standard carbon filter does not make unsafe water safe. Follow the advisory and boil, or use a filter certified for the contaminant named.

Filters also need maintenance. A spent filter stops working, and a neglected one can hold bacteria. Write the change date on the housing.

Whole-home or under-sink?

A whole-home system treats every tap, including shower water, and suits a household that dislikes the chloramine smell in a hot bath. An under-sink filter treats the drinking and cooking tap for a fraction of the cost and is easier to service.

Many homes do best with both a whole-home sediment stage and an under-sink carbon filter. We size the system to your flow rate so pressure stays steady.

Filter cartridges: change them on schedule

A filter has a rated capacity in gallons or months. Past that, it stops removing what it was built to remove. Set a calendar reminder when you install it, write the date on the housing and keep a spare set. A system you forget to maintain is worse than none, because you assume it is protecting you.

Read your utility's water quality report first

Columbia Water publishes an annual water quality report, also called a consumer confidence report. It lists what the utility found in treated water and compares it with federal limits. Read it before you buy a filter, so the equipment you choose answers something the report or your own tap actually shows.

If you are on another city's system, or on a private well, the same logic applies. Ask for the report or order a lab test, and match the filter to the result.

Reading Columbia Water's report before you buy

Columbia Water draws from the Broad River Diversion Canal and Lake Murray and publishes an annual water quality report. Use it to see the chloramine range before choosing media. In Lexington County, a water system or a well may have no report at all, which makes a lab test the right first step.

Filtration choices from Lake Murray to Lower Richland

Houses around Chapin get most of their water from the City of Columbia system, which treats with chloramine, so a carbon filter is the usual choice for taste. Blythewood's water comes from Winnsboro, so check that supplier's report before you pick equipment.

Well owners in Lower Richland need a test first, because sediment, iron and bacteria each call for a different system.
