Life on the Capital of Lake Murray

Chapin is in Lexington County, roughly 20 miles northwest of downtown Columbia. Lake Murray, formed by the Dreher Shoals Dam between 1927 and 1930, covers about 50,000 acres and has more than 500 miles of shoreline. A good share of the homes around Chapin are lake houses, second homes and retirement houses within sight of the water.

Lakefront ownership brings its own plumbing list. Outdoor spigots, irrigation lines and dock supply lines face sun and the occasional hard freeze. A house used only part of the year can sit for weeks with water standing in its lines, which is hard on fittings and seals. Dispatch is open around the clock, including holiday weekends when the lake houses are full.

Who runs your water and sewer in Chapin

The Town of Chapin owns and operates the entire sewer system. For water, the town owns the lines only in the Amicks Ferry Road area and on parts of Columbia Avenue, and the City of Columbia operates the remaining water lines.

That split decides who you call about a leak on the street side of the meter. Start with the utility on your bill, and if you are unsure, close the main valve inside the house and call us. New customers start service in person at the town's utilities office.

Before we arrive: a burst pipe or active leak at your Chapin home

Lake houses are often empty when a line lets go, and a small leak can run for days. If you are on site, take these four steps.

1

Close the main valve

Look near the water heater, in the garage or at the crawlspace entry for the valve where the line enters the house. In a house used part of the year, test it at every visit so it is not stuck on the night you need it. If it will not close, tell us, because the meter shutoff belongs to the utility.

2

Cut power near the water

Switch off breakers for wet circuits only if the panel is dry and you can reach it without stepping in water. Water running through a ceiling toward a light fixture is an electrical hazard, so stay out of that room. A wet panel is a reason to stay clear and say so on the call.

3

Protect what you can

Lift rugs, electronics and stored gear out of the wet area. Photograph the damage first, since a lake-house claim often involves more than one adjuster. Keep children and pets away from standing water.

Lake-house problems worth catching early

These four show up most often when a house is used in bursts.

A dripping outdoor spigot

A slow drip wastes water and can freeze and split the line behind it. Replacing the spigot is quick, and leak detection traces the pipe if the wet spot is not at the fitting.

A water heater in a part-time house

A tank left full for weeks collects sediment and corrodes its fittings. A flush or a repair often restores it.

Slow drains after a full weekend

Guests strain older lines. If it returns, drain cleaning clears the clog and a camera shows whether the pipe itself is failing.

Irrigation with no tested backflow device

Lawn systems tie into the house supply, and a failed assembly can let lawn water back into it. See backflow testing.

Septic tanks, wells and slopes near the shore

Not every home near the lake is on the town sewer. Some use septic tanks and a few rely on private wells, so know which system serves yours before you call. Plumbing on the house side of either system follows the International Plumbing Code (IPC) as South Carolina adopts it, plus any local rules.

Houses on slopes sometimes pump waste uphill with a grinder or ejector pump. When that pump fails, toilets and drains back up quickly, so treat an alarm as an emergency and call so dispatch can plan the visit.
