The valve and the drain are the parts that matter

Showers must have a pressure-balancing or thermostatic valve under the International Plumbing Code (IPC), which South Carolina adopts with state modifications. That protects the person in the shower when someone flushes a toilet elsewhere.

The drain connects to a trap and a vent. A drain without a proper vent gurgles and drains slowly. Replacing a drain in an older house is a chance to check what is behind the wall.

Waterproofing is the hidden step

Tile and grout are not waterproof. A membrane behind the tile and under the pan is what keeps the shower from rotting the framing. A flood test, filling the pan and checking for leaks over a day, should happen before tile goes up.

A slow leak behind a shower is a classic hidden problem. Our leak detection page explains how those are found when the signs finally show.

What older Columbia-area bathrooms hide

Opening a wall in a mid-century house in Forest Acres often reveals galvanized supply stubs and cast iron drains. Plan for the chance that some pipe needs replacing while the wall is open, and ask about repiping before the new tile covers it.

Permits and inspections

Changing a drain location, adding fixtures or moving supply lines usually needs a permit. The inspector looks at the rough-in before walls close. Skipping this can complicate a later home sale or an insurance claim.

Planning for safety and accessibility

If a bathroom may need to serve someone with limited mobility, plan blocking in the walls for grab bars and consider a low-threshold or walk-in shower. Doing it during the remodel costs far less than retrofitting later.

Where new showers leak

The common failures are a poorly sealed drain flange, a missing membrane at the corners and a valve body that was not supported in the wall. A flood test before tile catches the drain and pan problems. For the valve, pressure testing the supply lines catches leaks before drywall goes up.

Caulk, not grout, belongs in corners and where tile meets the tub. Flexible joints move with the house and keep water out.

What to do if a new shower leaks

A small leak behind tile spreads quickly, so act early.

  • 1Stop using the shower. Every use pushes more water into the wall or floor.
  • 2Note where the leak shows. Water at the ceiling below points to the drain or pan, while a wet wall points to the valve or tile.
  • 3Photograph and date the damage. Photos support a warranty claim or an insurance claim.
  • 4Call the installer and a plumber. A flood test of the pan and a pressure test of the supply lines separate the causes.

Bathroom work, permits and older houses

The International Plumbing Code with South Carolina changes calls for a 2-inch drain on a shower and a 1.5-inch drain on a tub, and a pressure-balancing valve. Richland County and Lexington County building offices decide when a remodel needs a permit. Opening walls in a mid-century house off Forest Drive often reveals galvanized stubs, and Columbia Water is soft, so scale is rarely the issue.

Flow limits and what they mean for the valve

Federal rules cap showerheads at 2.5 gallons per minute, and WaterSense models use 2.0 or less. A shower with several body sprays can exceed that, which raises the demand on your water heater and the size of the valve. Ask the installer to match the valve to the total flow you plan to use.

Tubs and showers in historic houses and new builds

Cast iron tubs in Elmwood Park and Shandon can weigh several hundred pounds, so removal and floor support are part of the job. The drain in such a house may also be old cast iron or galvanized pipe that needs checking before a new tub goes in.

In newer homes around Blythewood and Northeast Columbia, fiberglass and acrylic units set over a slab are the usual choice and go in faster. Either way, the valve and the drain size are checked against code first.
