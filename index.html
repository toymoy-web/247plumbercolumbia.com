What counts as a plumbing emergency

Some problems can wait for a weekday appointment. These cannot. A burst pipe can put hundreds of gallons into a house in an hour, so shutting off the main and calling a 24/7 emergency plumber comes first. Sewage coming up through a floor drain or tub means the main line is blocked, and drain cleaning or a camera look at the sewer line cannot wait.

A water heater that is leaking from the tank, not from a fitting, is usually finished, and a same-day water heater replacement is often the faster fix than repeated water heater repair visits. A sump pump that has stopped during heavy rain, a gas smell near an appliance, or water running under a slab with no visible source all deserve a call right away.

If you smell gas, leave the house and call 911 and the gas utility first. Then call us for the plumbing side.

Why Columbia plumbing fails the way it does

Columbia Water draws from two sources, the Broad River Diversion Canal and Lake Murray, and treats the water at two plants. The utility describes its water as 1 to 2 grains per gallon, which is on the soft side. That matters because many online guides blame hard water scale for every Columbia plumbing problem. With city water, scale is rarely the main culprit. Age and material usually are.

Chloramine is the other local factor. Columbia Water has used it as a disinfectant since the mid-1960s at the Canal plant and since 1982 at the Lake Murray plant. Chloramine is harder on some rubber parts than chlorine alone, so a toilet flapper, supply hose or water heater gasket that fails early is worth replacing with chloramine-resistant parts instead of the cheapest ones on the shelf.

Forest Acres is a good example of the first two rows. The city incorporated in 1935 and had water lines in place by 1939, and much of its housing is mid-century brick.

Homes like that often still have original galvanized supply pipe, which commonly runs out of life after 40 to 50 years. If you are seeing brown water or weak pressure, ask about repiping before you pay for several separate repairs. For hidden wet spots, leak detection finds the source without opening walls first.

Lessons from the October 2015 flood

In early October 2015, record rain hit the Columbia area. One gauge on Gills Creek in Forest Acres logged 10.64 inches in four hours, dams in the Gills Creek watershed failed, and the Columbia Canal breached, which led to a citywide boil-water advisory. Saturated ground pushes water into sump pits and sewer lines, and homes in low-lying spots feel it first.

The practical takeaway is cheap compared with the damage. If your home sits near Gills Creek, Rocky Branch or the Congaree floodplain, have a sump pump tested before storm season and ask whether a backwater valve belongs on your sewer line. Annual backflow testing matters too if you irrigate or run a commercial property.

What to do in the first ten minutes

  • 1Shut off the water. Turn the main valve clockwise. Know where it is before you need it.
  • 2Protect the electrics. If water is near outlets, panels or appliances, leave the breaker alone and stay out of standing water.
  • 3Stop the heat source. For a leaking water heater, switch off the power or the gas control and close the cold supply to the tank.
  • 4Photograph everything. Take photos and short video of the damage before you move anything, for your insurer or landlord.
  • 5Call (844) 981-1691. Tell us what you see, where the shutoff is, and whether anyone is at risk.

Homeowners policies often cover sudden events like a burst supply line and often exclude slow leaks that went unnoticed for months. Your policy language decides, so keep the photos and ask your insurer before you assume either way.
