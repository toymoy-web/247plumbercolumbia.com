My bill doubled and nothing is dripping: what is going on?

Most hidden leaks announce themselves indirectly. These are the common clues and what each one usually means.

A bill that jumps with no change in habits

Water is leaving the system somewhere after the meter. Irrigation, slab lines and toilets are the usual suspects.

The meter moves with every fixture off

That confirms a leak. The next question is whether it is inside the house or in the yard line.

A warm spot on the floor

A hot-water line under the slab can warm the surface above it.

Mildew smell, bubbling paint or a damp baseboard

Water is reaching a wall or floor from a line you cannot see.

A ten-minute meter test you can do yourself

Before you pay for anything, confirm there is a leak.

  • 1Turn off every fixture. Include the dishwasher, washer, ice maker and irrigation.
  • 2Find the meter. It is usually near the street in a ground box. Lift the lid carefully.
  • 3Read the leak indicator. Most meters have a small low-flow dial or triangle that spins when any water moves.
  • 4Wait fifteen minutes and look again. Any change in the reading means water is leaking.
  • 5Close the main and test again. If the meter stops, the leak is inside the house. If it keeps moving, it is in the line from the meter.

Where leaks hide in Columbia-area houses

Mid-century houses in Forest Acres often have galvanized supply lines and original water heater closets, which are common leak spots. Newer homes in Lexington more often leak at fixtures, hoses and water heater connections.

Yard irrigation is another frequent culprit in the Midlands, where lawns are watered hard through summer. A broken irrigation lateral or a stuck valve can run for weeks before the bill shows it.

Why pinpointing matters more than patching

Opening walls on a guess is expensive and often wrong. A precise location means a small opening, a targeted repair and less restoration. Keep the meter readings you took, because they help an insurer or utility understand the timeline of a leak.

Ask your utility whether it offers any adjustment on a bill inflated by a verified leak. Policies differ, and the repair invoice is usually part of the paperwork.

Emergency leak detection: when the leak cannot wait until morning

Some leaks are an emergency from the first minute. Water coming through a ceiling, a meter spinning with every fixture off, a pool of water on the lawn or a flooded garage all justify a call at any hour. Close the main valve first, then call so a technician can start finding the source while the damage is still small.

On the emergency line we ask where the water appears, whether the meter keeps moving after the main is closed and whether anything electrical is wet. Those three answers tell us if the leak is inside the house, in the yard line or in an appliance supply, and they decide which locating tools go on the truck.

If the water stops when you close the main and returns when you open it, the leak is on the house side of the valve. If the meter still moves with the main closed, the leak sits in the buried line before the house, and water line repair usually follows the pinpointing visit.

Questions callers ask before booking leak detection

People who call about hidden leaks tend to ask the same few things first. Do you have equipment that finds a leak inside a wall or under a slab? Will you mark the exact spot? What does the test cost on a typical house? Can the company that finds the leak also fix it?

We answer each one on the phone. Acoustic, thermal and pressure tools locate the leak without opening walls, the spot is marked on the floor or ground so the repair is a small opening, and the price is quoted before the visit. A homeowner who prefers to make the repair personally can take the location and do so.

Because the repair depends on what the finder uncovers, the report names the pipe, the likely depth and the probable fix. From there you choose. Our crew can make the repair, or you can hand the report to another contractor.

Water stains on ceilings and walls: tracing the source

A brown ring on a ceiling, bubbling paint or a damp patch on a bathroom wall points to water, yet the stain is rarely where the leak is. Water follows framing and pipes before it shows, so the source can sit a room away or a floor above.

Callers describe leaks coming through a dining room ceiling from the bathroom above, through a garage wall and around a hole already cut in drywall. Thermal imaging and moisture meters map the wet area first, then a pressure test on the suspect line confirms whether a supply pipe or a drain is the source.

Mark the edge of the stain with a pencil and the date, then watch whether it grows. A stain that grows only when someone showers points to a drain, valve or tub seal. One that grows constantly points to a pressurized supply line.

Leaks under the house: crawlspace and subfloor clues

Houses on crawlspaces hide a lot of plumbing. Water sounds at night, a musty smell, sagging insulation or a wet crawlspace floor can all mean a supply or drain leak below the floor. Some owners shut off the water because it sounds as though someone is showering under the house.

A technician checks supply lines, drain joints and the water heater connections from below and listens at accessible points. Cracked drain joints and sagging drain runs show up as damp soil or stained wood, while pinhole supply leaks show up as a steady drip or a green crust on copper.

Wear gloves if you look yourself, watch for animals and wiring, and stay out of standing water. If the space is wet and you cannot tell why, burst pipe repair handles the emergency fix and leak detection handles the diagnosis.

Outdoor leaks: sprinklers, hot tubs and the meter box

Many leak calls start outside. An irrigation valve that runs fast, a patch of lush weeds above a line, a soggy spot near a hot tub or a puddle around the meter box all suggest water escaping underground. The yard shows only the symptom, so the first step is isolating which line feeds the wet area.

Close the irrigation zone valves and the hot tub supply one at a time and watch the meter. When the meter stops after one is closed, that line is your leak. Backflow testing often turns up irrigation leaks too, because the assembly feeds the whole sprinkler system.

Irrigation, pools and other leaks that hide outside

Lawn irrigation is a common source of hidden water loss, because a broken lateral or a stuck zone valve can run for weeks. Try the meter test with the irrigation controller off, then with it on.

A pool with an autofill valve can also hide a leak behind the water level. The International Plumbing Code (IPC) requires new water piping to be pressure tested before it is concealed, and the same kind of test is how a technician proves an older line holds.

How much a small leak really wastes

The Environmental Protection Agency estimates that about 10 percent of homes have leaks wasting 90 gallons or more per day. A faucet dripping once per second can waste more than 3,000 gallons in a year. A toilet flapper that sticks open can lose water silently, which is why the dye test and the meter test come before any digging.

Four tests that narrow a hidden leak

Start with the meter, since a dial that moves with every fixture off confirms a leak after the meter. Then drop a few drops of food coloring into each toilet tank and wait without flushing. Color in the bowl means a leaking flapper.

Next, close the main valve for an hour and read the meter again, then close individual shutoffs to see which branch is losing water. A pressure gauge on a hose bib shows whether the system holds pressure. Each test removes part of the house from the search.

Reading your Columbia Water bill after a leak

Columbia Water bills in units of 100 cubic feet, and one unit is about 748 gallons, so a jump of a few units is a lot of water. Its customer guide describes a plumbing repair adjustment for customers whose usage returns to normal after a repair. The adjustment is limited to three months and one adjustment in any 12-month period.

Keep the plumber's invoice and the dates of the repair, since the utility reviews the account only after the repair is complete and usage has settled. The guide also points customers to its free EyeOnWater app for tracking daily use and setting leak alerts.

The usual hiding places for a leak

Many leaks sit in the places nobody looks. Washing machine hoses, ice-maker lines, toilet supply lines and water heater connections fail slowly and often leak onto a floor you do not inspect. Irrigation lines are another source, since a buried break can run for weeks.

Supply pipe inside walls and under slabs is harder to find, and the signs are indirect, such as a damp spot, a musty smell, warm floor tiles or the sound of running water at night. Tell the technician about any of these when you call, and which fixtures or appliances sit closest.

What the report should include

A useful leak detection report names the method used, the location of the leak in plain terms and the readings that support it. It also states which lines were tested and which were cleared.

Keep it with your records. If the leak led to damage, the report supports an insurance claim and helps a later buyer understand what was found and repaired.

Finding hidden leaks in century-old neighborhoods

Houses in Shandon, Earlewood and Elmwood Park have been repiped, patched and added onto over a century, so a hidden leak can sit behind plaster or under a later addition. Acoustic listening and a thermal camera narrow it to a small spot before any wall is opened.

Rural addresses in Hopkins and Chapin have the opposite problem, a long buried line. A meter test shows whether water is escaping between the meter and the house before anyone digs.
