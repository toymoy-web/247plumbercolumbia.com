A town built mostly in the last 30 years

Lexington grew 198 percent between the 1990 and 2000 censuses and another 83 percent by 2010. Nearly everything built in that rush went up after 1995, so expect PEX or CPVC supply lines, PVC drains and water heaters now in their tenth to fifteenth season.

That is the pattern behind most calls from newer Lexington homes. Tank water heaters from the building boom are reaching replacement age, and the International Plumbing Code (IPC) sets the rules for relief valves and discharge piping on every new install.

Tankless units need descaling, and added gas appliances need properly sized gas lines, which follow the International Fuel Gas Code (IFGC) as South Carolina adopts it. The pipes in the walls are usually fine. The things attached to them are not. Around the clock, dispatch takes Lexington calls for failed fittings and water heaters that cannot wait until morning.

Why the courthouse moved to this ridge

The county seat sat in Granby, where Cayce is now, until chronic flooding forced it out. In 1820 the courthouse moved to a high sand ridge near Twelve Mile Creek, and the Town of Lexington grew up around it. That is the reason flood preparation matters less here than it does in Cayce.

That history is useful to a homeowner. Much of the town sits on higher ground than the Congaree floodplain, so the emergencies here lean toward age, appliances and supply lines, not river water in the crawlspace. Low spots near creeks still need a working sump pump.

Before we arrive: a burst pipe or leaking water heater in Lexington

Newer Lexington homes usually have quarter-turn valves and plastic or PEX lines, which makes the first steps quick.

1

Quarter-turn the main

In a house built since the mid-1990s the main is usually a lever valve near the water heater or where the line enters the garage wall. Turn the lever across the pipe to close it. If the water heater is the leak, closing the cold supply valve on top of the tank also stops the flow.

2

Power and gas

If the heater is wet, switch an electric unit off at its breaker and turn a gas control knob to off. Do not relight a pilot yourself in a wet closet. If you smell gas, leave the house and call Dominion Energy at 800-815-0083 or 911.

3

Water first, warranty later

Move belongings out of the way, soak up standing water with towels and photograph the damage. Then find the model and serial number on the heater label, since a newer unit may carry a warranty. The paperwork can wait until the water stops.

Check the warranty before you pay

A house under ten years old may still carry a builder warranty on plumbing, and a water heater or tankless unit often has a manufacturer warranty on the tank or heat exchanger. Before you pay for a replacement part, find the model and serial number on the label and look up the coverage.

If the part is covered, you pay only for what the warranty leaves out. If water is already spreading, shut off the supply first and sort out the paperwork afterward. A call at midnight should never wait on a warranty search.

Downtown Lexington's brick core and mill houses

Disastrous fires on Main Street in 1894 and 1918 led to the brick buildings that form the historic downtown. The Lexington Manufacturing Company built a textile mill in 1890 with houses for its workers at the east end of Main Street, and a municipal water system was running by 1927. Homes in that core are the oldest in town, so ask for a pipe-material check and a camera look at the sewer lateral before a remodel.

Houses near the old core can be a hundred years old, with galvanized supply pipe and cast iron stacks. For those, repiping is the permanent fix for repeated leaks, and sewer line repair handles the clay laterals that roots like.

Where Lexington's water comes from

The town closed its own water plant on October 1, 1999 and tied into the City of West Columbia's plant. Outside the town system, the Joint Municipal Water and Sewer Commission serves unincorporated parts of the county, and some properties use private wells.

That matters for treatment. Homes on wells are the ones where a water softener or water filtration system most often makes sense, and a water test should come first. We work on the plumbing and treatment equipment inside the house. For the well pump itself, a licensed well contractor is the right call.
