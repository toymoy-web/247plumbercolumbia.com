Emergency Sump Pump Repair in Columbia, SC

A sump pump that quits during heavy rain turns a damp crawlspace into a flood. The fix is a working pump, a clear discharge line and a backup for the power outage that often comes with the storm.

What to do before the storm: a five-minute pump check

Do this when the weather is dry, and the pump is far likelier to work when it counts.

  • 1Pour in a bucket of water. The pump should start, empty the pit and shut off.
  • 2Check the float. It should rise and fall freely. A stuck float is the commonest failure.
  • 3Look at the discharge line. It should run well away from the foundation and not be crushed or frozen.
  • 4Test the check valve. It stops water from falling back into the pit when the pump stops.
  • 5Test the battery backup. A backup pump or battery that has not been checked in years may not run.

What the October 2015 flood taught the Midlands

During the October 2015 storm, a gauge on Gills Creek in Forest Acres recorded 10.64 inches of rain in four hours. At least four dams in the Gills Creek watershed failed, and the Columbia Canal breached. Saturated ground and power outages combined, which is the situation a battery backup is built for.

If your lot slopes toward Gills Creek, Rocky Branch or the Congaree floodplain, a backup is worth considering. Our location pages for Forest Acres and Cayce explain each area's flood pattern.

Installing a pump the right way

A good install starts with a sealed pit set below the floor, a pump sized to the water coming in, a check valve above the pump, and a discharge line that carries water away. The discharge should not feed back toward the house or into the sanitary sewer, which the local rules often forbid.

Where a house has no pit, a crawlspace or yard drain may need to be built first. See basement waterproofing for the drainage side of the same problem.

When the pump is not the problem

A pump that runs constantly may be handling a water line break, a high water table or a failed check valve. A pump that never runs may sit in a dry pit because the water found another path. A plumber looks at the whole system before replacing parts.

Winter and summer care for a sump system

In summer, thunderstorms make the pump run in bursts, so check it after the first big storm of the season. In winter, make sure the discharge line is not frozen or buried in leaves, because a blocked line backs water into the pit. Clean the pit of debris once a year and replace the battery on schedule.

Pumps near Gills Creek, Rocky Branch and the Congaree

Homes in Richland County near Gills Creek and Rocky Branch, and in Lexington County near the Congaree River, are where flood history makes pumps matter most. A battery backup covers the outages that storms bring. Your city or county sets where a discharge line may go, and local rules often forbid feeding it into the sanitary sewer that the South Carolina Department of Environmental Services regulates.

Sump pump repair: what each symptom means

Most sump pump repairs start with how the pump is behaving. Use this as a guide, then call if the fix is not obvious.

What you seeLikely causeUsual repair
Motor hums but no water movesJammed impeller or an airlocked pumpClear the impeller, add a small relief hole, or replace the pump
Pump never turns onNo power, a tripped outlet, a dead switch or a stuck floatReset the outlet, free the float, or replace the switch or pump
Pump runs nonstopFloat stuck up, failed check valve or a high water tableReposition the float, replace the check valve, or fix the drainage
Pump cycles every few secondsCheck valve missing or leaking, or a pit that is too smallFit a new check valve or resize the pit
Loud rattling or bangingDebris at the impeller or water hammer at the check valveClean the pit and replace the valve
Pit fills even though the pump runsPump undersized, or the discharge line is blocked or frozenClear or reroute the line, or upsize the pump

Repair or replace: how to decide

A pump under five years old with a stuck float or a bad switch is usually a repair. One past eight to ten years with a rusty housing, a burned motor or a cracked base is usually a replacement.

A replacement is also the right moment to add a battery backup and a high-water alarm. Both cost far less than the flooring they protect.

Safe checks while the storm is still going

These steps keep you safe and give dispatch useful information.

  • 1Stay out of standing water. Never reach into the pit while the pump is plugged in and water is high.
  • 2Check the outlet. Look at the breaker and the outlet reset button first, since a tripped circuit is the simplest cause.
  • 3Test the float from a dry spot. Stand on a dry surface and lift the float with a wooden handle. If the pump starts, the float was stuck.
  • 4Slow the water if you can. Bail with a bucket to keep the level below the floor, and keep away from any electrics.
  • 5Call and describe the sound. Humming, silence, rattling and nonstop running each point to a different repair.

Backup pumps and alarms: the repair most people skip

A battery backup only helps if the battery is alive. Batteries need replacing every few years, and many fail without warning because nobody tests them.

A high-water alarm costs little and gives you a warning before water reaches the floor. Ask for one at every pump repair.

What a sump pump repair visit includes

We start with the symptom and check power, the float, the pump, the check valve and the discharge line in that order. Most repairs end on one of those five parts.

Before any work you hear the price and the options. If the pit is cracked, undersized or filling from a leak, we explain that too, so the repair fixes the cause and not only the symptom.
